# Where do your ideas usually come from?
As an older creator once told me: **"Make what you want to kiss."**

I am a huge fan of reader-insert stories, OC-focused fandom fanfiction, and original characters. Because of that, most of my bots are dominant-coded characters who become strongly attached to, protective of, or obsessed with the user.

My ideas come from many random places, including:

- Adult doujinshi and games I discover on Pixiv
- YouTube Shorts about Y/N POV scenarios or novel tropes
- AI-generated vlogs, especially isekai-style vlogs
- Japanese gameplay videos
- Interesting environments, locations, or everyday situations
- News stories and social media posts

For example, I created my **Soaked Navarro Family** bot after walking past a toy store displaying NERF water guns.

Another bot, **Rafael — Missed Flight**, was inspired by a story I saw on Twitter/X about two people who missed their flight and had to share one hotel room.

Inspiration can come from almost anywhere.

# How do you decide whether an idea is worth building?
I usually ask myself two things:

1. Does the idea fit my personal interests?
2. Has someone already made the same concept many times?

For example, CEO romance is extremely popular, so there is a lot of competition between bots using the same basic trope. That does not mean I cannot create another CEO bot, but I need to give it a distinct identity.

Even when I use a familiar trope, I try to introduce a different genre, conflict, setting, personality dynamic, or story mechanic so that the bot has its own flavor instead of feeling like another version of the same concept.

# Do you start with a character, a world, or a story?
I usually start with either the character or the setting, depending on which idea comes to me first.

As an OC artist, I love designing original characters. Bot creation allows me to combine that love with storytelling and let other people interact with my OCs. However, sometimes the world or setting appears in my mind first, and I create the character around it.

My process usually looks like this:

1. Create the main character concept
2. Decide on the primary trope or genre
3. Add supporting tropes or conflicts
4. Build the setting and world around the character

For quicker or more self-contained bots, I may not develop a large setting. Sometimes a strong character and an interesting combination of tropes are enough.

**Additional context**

I try not to make a bot rely on only one trope or genre. I prefer combining different elements to create something fresher.

For example, I mixed:

- CEO romance
- Comedy
- A retired Demon Lord
- Enemies-to-lovers tension

That combination eventually became my bot **My CEO Is a Retired Demon Lord**.

# How do you avoid making characters feel generic?
I usually begin by giving an AI assistant the main elements of the character, the tropes and genres I am using, and the kind of story I want to tell. I then ask how the concept could be made more distinctive, immersive, or internally consistent.

Most of my characters are dominant or top-coded because my bots are generally designed for submissive or bottom-coded users like myself. However, I do not want that dynamic to completely restrict how users interact with them.

Many users enjoy teasing, challenging, or eventually taking control of my dominant characters. A well-designed character should be flexible enough to support different user approaches without suddenly becoming out of character.

When you understand and care about the character, it becomes easier to make them flexible without losing consistency.

# What is your favorite method for brainstorming?
Most of my brainstorming process is already covered in my answer about [where my ideas come from.]{.underline}

I collect interesting scenes, tropes, characters, environments, and situations from different sources. Then I ask myself how I could combine them into something that suits my interests and feels different from existing bots.

# What details make a character feel "alive"?
It depends on what kind of character and interaction you want to create.

Because most of my characters are dominant or top-coded, I design them from the perspective of someone who enjoys playing a submissive or bottom-coded user.

I want the character to love, desire, protect, or become obsessed with the user, but they still need their own:

- Personality
- Values
- Habits
- Flaws
- Emotional reactions
- Boundaries
- Speaking style
- Personal goals
- Story-specific quirks

A character feels more alive when their affection does not erase their personality. They should care about the user in a way that is unique to who they are.

# What is one prompt-writing technique you wish more creators knew?
When your character prompt feels bloated and you do not know what to remove, give it to an AI assistant and use an instruction such as:

[Make this prompt more concise without removing any important elements or weakening the character's and setting's identities.]

The AI can show you where information is being repeated, where sentences can be compressed, and which details are not contributing much to the bot's behavior.

However, always review the result yourself. Concise does not automatically mean better if the revision removes the details that make your character distinctive.

# What common prompt mistakes do you see?
One common issue is using {{user}} too frequently in the character prompt.

This can become especially noticeable when a greeting is written entirely in second-person POV. Because the greeting becomes part of the model's starting context, the AI may interpret the second-person narration as a style it should continue using. This can sometimes cause it to narrate the user's thoughts, actions, or dialogue.

For example:

{{user}} is {{char}}'s girlfriend. She has dated him for two years.

Depending on the model, repeatedly defining the user this way may encourage the AI to treat the user like another character it is allowed to control.

When possible, I mention {{user}} only where necessary. After establishing the relationship, I use a role or title instead.

For example:

{{user}} is {{char}}'s master.
{{char}} obeys his master loyally and constantly seeks their approval.

This reduces unnecessary repetition and may help reinforce the intended separation between the character and the user.

I also try to avoid greetings that decide too many things for the user. Most users do not enjoy having the AI control their dialogue, emotions, decisions, or reactions before they have even responded.

# How do you organize lore?
I am honestly not a very organized person, lol.

I usually write down the individual pieces of lore I want and give them to an AI assistant. I ask it to help connect the ideas, identify contradictions, and organize everything into a structure that makes sense.

Then I review the result, make adjustments, and continue consulting the AI until the world matches what I imagined.

I often make final changes shortly before publishing because I tend to notice missing details only after seeing the entire bot assembled.

# How do you write emotional scenes?
By feeling the scene myself, I guess? I do not consider myself especially good at writing angst, but I enjoy working with it sometimes. I often use the classic approach of **giving the characters hope, then pulling it away**.

Either `{{user}}` or `{{char}}` must struggle, make difficult choices, and fight for the true ending they deserve. For me, emotional scenes work best when happiness is possible but not guaranteed. The characters should have something meaningful to lose, and reaching the true ending should feel earned rather than easily given.

# What dialogue habits do you actively avoid?
I do not enjoy greetings written in a format like this:

{{char}}: Hey, babe. *smiles* Ready to go home?

I prefer immersive prose that combines narration, action, atmosphere, and dialogue.

Instead of presenting the character like a basic chat script, I want the greeting to feel like the opening scene of an interactive story. It should give the user something meaningful to observe, react to, or respond to.

# How do you test a bot before publishing?
Sometimes I test extensively, and sometimes I do not, especially when the bot is designed mainly for a simple adult-focused scenario.

However, I always test bots with:

- Deeper lore
- Complex settings
- Multiple characters
- Progression systems
- Percentage meters
- Trackers
- Hidden information
- Long-term story objectives

I usually test the bot with several custom LLMs to see which model handles the prompt best.

During testing, I check whether the bot:

- Maintains its personality
- Continues the story instead of abandoning it
- Uses trackers correctly
- Avoids rushing progression
- Does not immediately force an adult scene
- Keeps secrets hidden until the correct moment
- Follows character-specific behavioral rules
- Handles multiple characters without confusing them

Testing is especially important when the bot depends on mechanics rather than only personality and dialogue.

# What is your biggest indicator that a bot is ready?
For me, a bot is ready when I have completed the main creation workflow and its behavior is close enough to what I intended.

It does not need to be absolutely perfect. There will always be something that could be rewritten, expanded, or visually improved.

The important part is that the character's identity, central scenario, behavior, and presentation all work together. If the remaining issues are minor and do not significantly affect the overall experience—aside from occasional differences in how each LLM interprets or processes the prompt—I consider the bot ready to publish.

# What is your debugging and creation workflow?
**My workflow usually looks like this:**

**1. Initial idea**

First, an idea strikes me, and I decide which tropes or genres I want to use.

**2. Early concept development**

I create a rough draft and think about how to make the concept feel less generic. If the bot is lore-heavy, I may begin drafting the setting, factions, history, rules, or mechanics during this stage.

**3. Character card**

Next, I build the character card, including the character's personality, background, relationships, behavior, speech style, and scenario-specific rules. However, Steps 2 and 3 can happen in either order. It depends on whether the setting or the character idea feels stronger and develops more quickly.

**4. Naming**

Choosing names is surprisingly difficult because AI assistants often recommend the same names repeatedly. You will probably see names such as:

- Kael
- Lucian
- Cassian
- Alaric
- Zephyr etc

These names are not necessarily bad, but they are so frequently suggested that they may make a character feel AI-generated.

There is also the possibility of accidentally choosing a name already associated with a real person, established character, creator, brand, or community project.

I once became involved in a conflict because a GPT name was already strongly associated with another creator. Accidental overlap is often harmless, but it can still create confusion when people recognize the existing name.

If a name becomes a genuine concern, I usually change or rearrange it. Sometimes adjusting one or two letters is enough. For example, I might change **Mateo** to **Mattheo**.

**5. Image generation**

After finishing the character card and setting, I move on to image generation.

This stage takes a lot of time because I am very picky and perfectionistic.

When possible, I try to avoid generic poses where the character simply leans slightly toward the viewer, tilts their head, and has a neutral expression.

My favorite visual style and posing can be seen in my **Lustweave** series. Even those images still require corrections because image models often struggle with faces when a character is shown from the waist up or placed in a dynamic pose.

**6. HTML and presentation**

For the bot page, I usually reuse HTML from one of my previous bots and ask an AI assistant such as GPT or Claude to adapt it.

I may request:

- A different color palette
- A new visual atmosphere
- A genre-specific design
- A rewritten synopsis
- A layout that better matches the current character

Claude often works especially well for this part.

**7. Image hosting and thumbnails**

I have experienced problems with image hosting in the past, so I now try to make sure I will not suddenly lose access to my bot images.

I also create the bot's profile thumbnail and add a watermark.

For multi-character bots, I may need to create a collage manually in Canva. I sometimes ask GPT to help first, but if the result does not look good, I eventually edit it myself in Canva.

**8. Model recommendations and final checks**

Finally, I decide which custom models to recommend for the bot.

Then I review the character card, images, HTML, formatting, and model behavior one final time.

Aaaand then it is finally done.

# What advice from another creator helped you most?
As a creator who enjoys making multi-character bots with long-context memory, my advice is not to let other people's skepticism prevent you from creating what you enjoy.

Memory limits can be studied. You can research the platform's context limits, learn how different LLMs process prompts, and optimize your information accordingly.

You may also encounter people who repeatedly ask for your private prompts or try to pressure you by saying:

"It is AI, so it is not original."

I disagree with that view. **Using an AI tool does not remove the [human effort]{.underline} behind creating a bot.** The model may generate the responses, but the creator still develops and manages:

- The original concept
- Character design
- Personality
- Lore
- Worldbuilding
- Scenarios
- Dialogue style
- Behavioral rules
- Progression systems
- Formatting
- Testing
- Revisions
- Visual presentation

A well-developed bot **[can take days or weeks]{.underline}** of planning, testing, rewriting, and adjustment before it behaves as intended.

People unfamiliar with bot creation may not immediately see how much work it involves.

Even when you feel like your bot is not accomplishing anything, remember that you are allowed to create something simply because you want it to exist. If you continue experimenting, you will eventually find your starting point.

My very first bot was about a male ghost living in a haunted apartment and having adult encounters with {{user}}, lol.

Without that first bot, there would be no **Valanadesu**, no connected lore, and none of the character- and worldbuilding-focused bots that people now enjoy.

Everyone starts somewhere.

# What kind of community resources would you love to see?
I would love to see a reliable prompt-watermarking or ownership-signature feature.

I once found information about a possible method on another website, but it was difficult to test and use properly.

Ideally, creators could embed a hidden ownership message inside their prompts. If a scraping website extracted and republished the prompt, a message such as the following could appear:

**This bot belongs to [Creator Name]. If you find it published elsewhere without permission, it may have been stolen.**

I would love to see platforms develop better tools for:

- Identifying scraped prompts
- Proving original ownership
- Reporting stolen bots
- Protecting private character cards
- Preventing unauthorized prompt extraction

These features would help creators protect their work while still allowing them to share their bots with the community.
